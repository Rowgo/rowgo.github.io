Tipsy Turtles

Tipsy Turtles is a 3rd person cooperative driving game where you and a friend play as conjoined turtles, Wee and Woo, trying to save a fish from polluted waters.

Myself and four others made Tipsy Turtles over the course of five months for our final project at Vancouver Film School. We also worked with two other students from the sound design campus for the SFX and music. On the team, I was responsible for designing and implementing the UI for the game, managing the project, creating the visual effects, putting together the cinematics, and documenting the game's core mechanics.

Fish Bowl UI

The core of our game was in the obtuse movement controls that the players had to overcome as a team to progress. When we researched other games with obtuse movement controls we found that what made those games so memorable were the silly mistakes that came from using unintuitive controls. However, unlike most of those games, our game didn't have a failure state attached to misinputs, because of this we were worried players would take the game slowly to prevent themselves from making mistakes. Ultimately taking the fun out of the game. In order to force players into making those silly mistakes we added a time pressure element to the game that pushed players to move quickly. This time pressure element was having the fishbowl that Wee and Woo were transporting leak over time, which also helped tie the narrative of our game into the gameplay.

I was tasked with designing and implementing the UI and gameplay for both the fishbowl and check points, and one of the decisions I made early on was that the UI for the fishbowl should be diegetic. This was because the fishbowl would always be in the center of the screen, and anything we did for the UI to show that the fishbowl was running out of water would also need to be done for the fishbowl's in-game model, to maintain the game's immersion. Making the UI diegetic prevented us from duplicating our work, and allowed us to put more effort into making the fishbowl look as good as it could, in the short time frame that we had. This was important since the fishbowl was a core part of our gameplay, and our narrative.

After creating the A-Z of our game we realized that the slightly overhead view we had originally planned for didn't give players the right angle to clearly see where the water level of the fishbowl was. To fix this, I proposed to the team that we tilt the camera down to be directly behind the characters. After discussing it, we decided to move forward with the change, much to the dismay of our environment artist, (sorry Jaylon).

But we ran into another problem. Even though the fish bowl was in the center of the screen, we found that during our blind playtests players didn't notice the fishbowl, or that it was leaking. This would cause players to lose the game without understanding why. To draw more attention to the fishbowl I talked to Mim (the character artist) and asked if she could create some animations for the fish. After she got them blocked out I hooked them up so that they would play when the fishbowl got low. After that I added some splashing effects, and made them white to create high contrast and grab the attention of our players. This worked, and players were now noticing the fishbowl in our blind playtests. Buuuuuut I noticed that now there was confusion around when the fishbowl was full, so I talked to Mim again to get one more animation made to play for when players had finished filling the fishbowl.

Making our UI diegetic came with a lot of challenges, but in the end I'm happy with the choice we made. It gave us the opportunity to inject a lot of personality into the fish's character and allowed players to connect more with the game as a result.
